# Evidence hierarchy — how much to trust a source

Use this to weight sources when synthesizing. Higher tiers dominate lower ones
when they conflict. Always weight by **methodological quality within a tier**,
not tier alone — a large, pre-registered RCT can outweigh a weak meta-analysis
of poor studies.

## Tiers (strongest → weakest)

1. **Systematic reviews & meta-analyses** of high-quality studies (Cochrane,
   Campbell, well-conducted PRISMA reviews). Living systematic reviews and
   umbrella reviews sit here too.
2. **Consensus statements from authoritative scientific bodies** — WHO, IPCC,
   national academies of science, major professional societies, government
   statistical agencies. These represent expert-aggregated consensus.
3. **Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)** and, in non-clinical fields,
   pre-registered controlled experiments with adequate power.
4. **Quasi-experimental & cohort studies**, natural experiments, large
   longitudinal observational studies.
5. **Case-control and cross-sectional studies**, smaller observational work.
6. **Case reports, case series, mechanistic/animal/in-vitro studies** —
   hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory.
7. **Expert opinion, narrative reviews, editorials, textbooks** — useful for
   background and framing, weak for specific factual claims.
8. **Preprints** (arXiv, bioRxiv, medRxiv, SSRN) — not yet peer-reviewed. Cite
   with an explicit "preprint, not peer-reviewed" flag; upgrade the claim once a
   peer-reviewed version exists.

## Quality signals to check inside any tier

- **Replication**: has the finding been independently reproduced? A single
  striking result is provisional.
- **Sample size & power**; pre-registration; conflicts of interest / funding
  source; effect size and confidence intervals (not just p-values).
- **Recency vs. landmark status**: prefer current evidence, but recognise
  foundational work a field still relies on.
- **Retraction status**: check the source has not been retracted or issued a
  correction (Retraction Watch / Crossref).
- **Citations in context**: highly-cited ≠ correct; look at *how* it is cited
  (supported, disputed, superseded).

## GRADE-style summary language

When stating confidence, use calibrated wording:
- **High certainty** — consistent high-quality evidence; consensus.
- **Moderate certainty** — generally consistent but with limitations.
- **Low certainty** — sparse, conflicting, or low-quality evidence.
- **Very low / contested** — active scientific debate; state both sides.
